Building in Thailand, whether it is a family house, a small villa project or a condominium, means working through several sets of rules at once: who owns or leases the land, what the local zoning allows, which permits the building needs, how the land can be divided and sold, and what the construction contract says when something goes wrong. Each step is manageable on its own. Most problems come from doing them in the wrong order, or from assuming that a contractor or agent has already handled the legal side.

We advise landowners, developers, investors and contractors on the legal side of a project from the first site visit to handover, and after.

Before you buy or build: checking the land

The title deed decides almost everything that follows. A Chanote (Nor Sor 4 Jor) is a fully surveyed freehold title; lesser documents such as Nor Sor 3 Gor carry more risk and limit what can be done with the land. We check the deed and its history at the Land Office, registered encumbrances such as mortgages, leases, servitudes and usufructs, and whether the land has legal road access.

We also check the town plan. Comprehensive city plans issued under the City Planning Act divide land into colour-coded zones that control use, building type, height and the share of a plot that can be built on. Coastal and hillside areas, including much of Phuket and Pattaya, can carry extra restrictions on height and slope. It is far cheaper to find a restriction before a purchase than after a design has been paid for.

Building permits

Construction, alteration and demolition of a building are controlled by the Building Control Act B.E. 2522 (1979) and the ministerial regulations under it. In most cases you need a permit from the local authority (the municipality or Tambon Administrative Organisation) before work starts. The application is supported by plans and calculations signed by licensed architects and engineers.

The Act also allows a notification route, where licensed professionals certify the design and the owner notifies the authority instead of waiting for a permit. It can save time, but it shifts responsibility onto the owner and the professionals who sign, so it suits experienced teams with clean designs.

Building without a permit, or departing from the approved plans, can lead to orders to stop work, fines and, in serious cases, orders to modify or demolish. Unpermitted work also causes problems later, when you try to register a house, sell, mortgage or insure the property.

Subdividing land and selling plots

Dividing land into plots for sale is regulated by the Land Allocation Act B.E. 2543 (2000). Where land is divided into ten or more plots for sale, or divided in stages that add up to that, the developer generally needs a land allocation permit from the provincial Land Allocation Committee before selling. The permit process covers the layout, roads, drainage, utilities and the arrangements for maintaining common areas once plots are sold.

Projects that try to stay under the threshold, for example by dividing in small batches or through related companies, can be treated as a single allocation. If they are, buyers' contracts and the developer's position can be at risk. We advise on whether a project needs a permit and, if it does, on preparing the application and the sale documents to match it.

Condominiums and larger developments

A building that will be sold as individual units under the Condominium Act B.E. 2522 (1979) must be registered as a condominium, with a juristic person set up to manage common property. Foreign ownership of units is allowed within the legal quota for each building, which affects how units are marketed and sold.

Larger projects may also need an environmental impact assessment (EIA) under the Enhancement and Conservation of National Environmental Quality Act. Whether an EIA is required depends on the type and size of the project, and approval can take months, so it needs to be built into the timetable from the start.

Construction contracts

In Thai law a construction contract is usually a contract for hire of work under the Civil and Commercial Code. The Code supplies default rules, but the written contract does most of the work. We draft and review owner and contractor agreements, design-and-build contracts, consultant appointments and subcontracts, in English, Thai or both. On larger projects we work with FIDIC-based forms adapted to Thai law.

The clauses that matter most are usually:

  • a clear scope, drawings and specification, so variations can be priced fairly;
  • payment stages tied to measurable progress, with retention held until defects are fixed;
  • completion dates, extensions of time and liquidated damages for delay;
  • who obtains which permits and who bears the cost of approvals;
  • defects liability, warranties and insurance;
  • termination rights, and how disputes are resolved (negotiation, mediation, arbitration or the Thai courts).

The Code also makes a contractor responsible for defects that appear within one year of handover, or within five years for buildings constructed on or in the land other than wooden ones, unless the defect was concealed by the contractor, in which case liability can be longer. A good contract builds on these rules rather than leaving them to chance.

When a project goes wrong

Common disputes include late completion, defective work, unpaid progress claims, variations that were never agreed in writing, and contractors who leave the site. Early advice often keeps a dispute small: checking what the contract actually says, putting notices in writing, recording the condition of the work, and protecting your position on payments and retention.

Where a dispute cannot be settled, we represent owners and contractors in mediation, in arbitration under the Arbitration Act B.E. 2545 (2002), and in the Thai courts. See our Dispute Resolution page for how that process works.

Foreign owners and developers

Foreigners generally cannot own land in Thailand, but there are lawful ways to hold buildings, long leases and development rights, and to own a development company within the Foreign Business Act. Structures that rely on Thai nominee shareholders are under active scrutiny and can expose everyone involved. Frequently asked questions

Do I need a building permit to build a house on my own land?

In most areas, yes. Where the Building Control Act applies, a new house needs a permit from the local authority, or a valid notification under the Act, before construction starts. The application needs plans signed by licensed professionals. Building without one can lead to fines and orders to stop or modify the work, and causes problems when you later register, sell or mortgage the property.

How many plots can I sell before I need a land allocation permit?

The Land Allocation Act generally applies when land is divided into ten or more plots for sale, including when the division happens in stages. Splitting land between related companies or in small batches does not necessarily avoid the Act. If you plan to divide and sell land, it is worth checking before you start marketing plots.

Who is responsible for defects after the building is handed over?

Under the Civil and Commercial Code, the contractor is responsible for defects that appear within one year of handover, or within five years for buildings on or in the land other than wooden structures. The contract can add warranties, retention and insurance on top of these rules. Notify the contractor in writing as soon as a defect appears.

Can a foreigner develop property in Thailand?

A foreigner cannot usually own land, but can own buildings, hold long registered leases and, within the Foreign Business Act, own or invest in a development company. Condominium units can be owned within the foreign quota. The right structure depends on the project, so it should be settled before land is bought.

Should our construction contract be in English or Thai?

Often both. Thai authorities and courts work in Thai, and many contractors are more comfortable in Thai, while foreign owners and lenders need an English version they can rely on. A bilingual contract should say which language prevails if the two versions differ.
